A bit about me

Radiotherapy is a key treatment for lung cancer, but radiation damage to healthy lung can leave patients short of breath and reduce their quality of life. Functional lung avoidance radiotherapy addresses this by steering radiation away from the best-functioning lung regions, helping patients keep breathing well after treatment without compromising tumour control. The heart is also at risk: radiation can cause regional damage to the heart’s blood supply, and both baseline heart function and heart dose have been shown to affect patient survival. However, mapping lung and heart function currently requires three separate nuclear medicine scans with different tracers, which is costly, time-consuming, and rarely done in practice.

My research obtains lung ventilation, lung perfusion, and myocardial perfusion from a single dynamic contrast-enhanced CT scan, which can be acquired during the routine treatment planning CT by adding contrast, and I have shown in animal studies that it agrees with established reference standards. This would allow radiotherapy plans to protect both lung and heart function, and enable follow-up toxicity assessment for early detection and prompt intervention. Because it needs only a CT scanner and standard iodinated contrast rather than radioactive tracers, it would also make functional avoidance radiotherapy accessible to centres without nuclear medicine infrastructure.

Alongside my thesis, I work on myocardial perfusion from coronary CTA and on stroke perfusion imaging; see the research page. Before Western, I studied Systems and Biomedical Engineering at Cairo University and worked on medical-imaging AI at Sejong University. Outside the lab, I enjoy climbing, hiking, running, camping and other outdoor activities, as well as travelling, cooking, gaming, anime, and movies.
